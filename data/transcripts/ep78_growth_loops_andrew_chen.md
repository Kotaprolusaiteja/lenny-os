Lenny: Today I'm talking with Andrew Chen, who's a general partner at Andreessen Horowitz and the author of "The Cold Start Problem." We're going to be diving deep into growth loops. Andrew, welcome.

Andrew Chen: Thanks, Lenny. Growth loops are something I'm really passionate about because they're the fundamental building block of how products grow, and yet they're widely misunderstood.

Lenny: Let's start from the top. What exactly is a growth loop?

Andrew Chen: A growth loop is a closed system where the output of one cycle feeds directly into the input of the next cycle, creating compounding growth. The key difference between a growth loop and a funnel is that a funnel is linear — you pour users in the top and some come out the bottom. A growth loop is circular — users who come out the bottom generate more inputs at the top. The classic example is a viral loop. A user signs up, invites friends, some of those friends sign up, and they invite more friends. Each cycle generates more inputs than the previous one.

Lenny: Can you break down the main types of growth loops?

Andrew Chen: There are really three major categories. First, viral loops — where existing users bring in new users through direct invitations, shared content, or word of mouth. Think Dropbox's referral program or how Figma files get shared. Second, content loops — where user-generated content gets indexed by search engines and brings in new users. Pinterest is the canonical example. Users pin content, that content gets indexed by Google, new users discover it through search, they sign up and pin more content. Third, paid loops — where revenue from existing users funds acquisition of new users. This is how most enterprise SaaS companies grow. Each customer pays enough that you can reinvest a portion into acquiring the next customer.

Lenny: What makes a growth loop actually work versus just being a theoretical concept on a whiteboard?

Andrew Chen: Great question. There are three things that determine whether a growth loop actually works. First, the conversion rate at each step of the loop. If your viral loop has a step where only 1 percent of people convert, the loop is going to stall out quickly. You need to optimize every step. Second, the cycle time — how long it takes to complete one full loop. A growth loop that takes 6 months to complete is much less powerful than one that completes in a day. Third, and this is the one people miss, the amplitude — how many new inputs each cycle generates. If each user only generates 0.5 new users, you have a decaying loop. You need a viral coefficient above 1.0 for true viral growth, which is extremely rare.

Lenny: What do most companies get wrong about growth?

Andrew Chen: The biggest mistake is thinking about growth as a department rather than as a product feature. Growth isn't something you bolt on after you've built the product. The best growth loops are deeply embedded in the product experience. Slack didn't grow because they had a great marketing team. They grew because the product itself had a built-in viral loop — every time someone shared a channel with a colleague, that was growth happening inside the product. Another mistake is focusing too much on top-of-funnel acquisition and not enough on activation and retention. If you're losing 95 percent of signups in the first week, no amount of acquisition spending will save you. Fix the bucket before you pour more water in.

Lenny: How should an early-stage startup think about growth?

Andrew Chen: For early-stage startups, the most important thing is to find one growth loop that works and double down on it. Don't try to run five different loops simultaneously. Find the one that has the best unit economics and the shortest cycle time, and put all your energy into optimizing it. At the early stage, you should be spending 80 percent of your growth effort on improving retention and activation, and only 20 percent on acquisition. The reason is that improving retention has a compounding effect. If you improve monthly retention from 60 percent to 70 percent, that dramatically changes your 12-month retention curve. It's the single highest-leverage thing you can do.

Lenny: What about network effects? How do they relate to growth loops?

Andrew Chen: Network effects and growth loops are related but distinct concepts. A network effect means the product gets more valuable as more people use it. A growth loop is a mechanism that brings more people to the product. They're complementary. The most powerful products have both — a growth loop that brings in new users and a network effect that makes the product stickier as the user base grows. Uber is a great example. The growth loop was: more riders attract more drivers, which reduces wait times, which attracts more riders. The network effect was: more drivers in a geography meant shorter wait times for everyone, making the product fundamentally better. When you have both working together, you get what I call a "flywheel" — a self-reinforcing system that accelerates over time.

Lenny: Any final advice for product leaders thinking about growth?

Andrew Chen: Three things. First, measure everything. You can't optimize a growth loop if you don't know where the leaks are. Instrument every step, track conversion rates, track cycle times. Second, think in terms of cohorts, not aggregates. Your overall metrics might look flat, but if your newest cohort is retaining better than previous cohorts, you're actually improving. Third, be patient. Real growth loops take time to build and optimize. Don't chase vanity metrics or growth hacks. Build something sustainably and compound over time.
