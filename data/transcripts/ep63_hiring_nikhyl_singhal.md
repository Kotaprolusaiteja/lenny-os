Lenny: My guest today is Nikhyl Singhal, who's been a VP of Product at Meta, Credit Karma, and Google. He's one of the most thoughtful people I know on the topic of hiring and developing product talent. Nikhyl, welcome.

Nikhyl Singhal: Thanks, Lenny. Hiring is something I've spent an enormous amount of time on throughout my career. At Meta alone, I probably did over 500 PM interviews, and the patterns I've seen have really shaped how I think about talent.

Lenny: Let's start with the basics. What do great product hires have in common?

Nikhyl Singhal: After doing hundreds of interviews and managing dozens of PMs, I've identified what I call the "three H's" of great product people: Horsepower, Humility, and Hunger. Horsepower is raw cognitive ability — can they think through complex problems quickly? Can they see around corners? Humility is the willingness to be wrong, to listen to feedback, and to put the team's success above their own ego. Hunger is the relentless drive to make things better, to ship great products, to learn and grow. The most common failure mode I see in PM hiring is over-indexing on horsepower and under-indexing on humility. Brilliant PMs who can't collaborate end up doing more harm than good.

Lenny: How do you actually assess these qualities in an interview?

Nikhyl Singhal: For horsepower, I use product design questions but with a twist. I don't care about the candidate's final answer nearly as much as I care about their thinking process. I want to see them break down a vague problem into structured components. I want to see them consider multiple user segments. I want to see them identify trade-offs and make principled decisions. For humility, I ask about failures and disagreements. "Tell me about a product decision you were wrong about." Great candidates have specific, thoughtful answers. They don't blame others, and they can articulate what they learned. Weak candidates either can't think of one or tell a story where it's clear they still think they were right. For hunger, I look at what they've done outside of work. Have they built anything? Written anything? Mentored anyone? The best PMs I've hired are people who can't NOT build things.

Lenny: What's your approach to structuring PM interviews?

Nikhyl Singhal: I recommend a structured interview process with clear rubrics. Here's what I used at Meta. Round one: product sense — give them a product design question and assess their ability to define the problem, identify user segments, generate solutions, and make trade-offs. Round two: execution — give them a scenario about a product launch that's going wrong and see how they diagnose problems, prioritize actions, and communicate with stakeholders. Round three: strategy — give them a market landscape and ask them to develop a product strategy. Look for their ability to analyze competitive dynamics, identify opportunities, and think long-term. Round four: leadership and collaboration — behavioral questions about how they've worked with engineering, design, and data teams. This four-round structure covers the full spectrum of PM skills and reduces the risk of any single bias affecting the outcome.

Lenny: What about hiring for different PM levels?

Nikhyl Singhal: This is important because the job of a PM changes dramatically at different levels. For entry-level PMs, I'm looking primarily for raw potential — horsepower, curiosity, and the ability to learn quickly. They don't need to have product experience. Some of the best PMs I've hired at the entry level came from consulting, engineering, or even unrelated fields like journalism. For mid-level PMs, I'm looking for execution ability — can they take a problem, scope it, work with a team, and ship a solution? They should have a track record of shipping things that users actually value. For senior PMs and above, I'm looking for strategic thinking and leadership ability. Can they set direction for a product area? Can they influence without authority? Can they build and develop a team? The biggest mistake companies make is promoting great executors into leadership roles without checking whether they can actually lead.

Lenny: How do you think about diversity in PM hiring?

Nikhyl Singhal: Diversity is essential for building great products, and I don't mean that in a hand-wavy way. Diverse teams literally build better products because they can see problems from more angles. They catch blind spots that homogeneous teams miss. At Meta, some of our biggest product improvements came from team members who had personal experience with problems that the rest of the team hadn't even considered. Practically, building a diverse PM team requires intentional effort at every stage. It means sourcing candidates from non-traditional backgrounds. It means structuring interviews to minimize bias. It means creating an inclusive culture where people feel safe to share different perspectives. It means looking at your promotion and retention data to make sure the pipeline is working for everyone.

Lenny: Any final advice for companies trying to build great PM teams?

Nikhyl Singhal: Three things. First, invest in PM development, not just PM hiring. Many companies spend months recruiting great PMs and then do nothing to help them grow once they join. Create mentorship programs, provide coaching, and give PMs stretch opportunities. Second, create a clear career ladder with specific, measurable expectations at each level. This removes ambiguity and helps PMs understand what they need to do to advance. Third, don't be afraid to hire unconventional candidates. Some of the best PMs I've ever worked with didn't have traditional PM backgrounds. They were engineers who discovered they loved talking to customers, or designers who realized they wanted to own the strategy. Great product instinct can come from anywhere.
