Lenny: Welcome back to Lenny's Podcast. I'm here today with Lauryn Isford, who leads Growth at Airtable and previously worked at Notion. We're going to be talking about onboarding, which is one of the most underinvested areas in product development. Lauryn, welcome.

Lauryn Isford: Thanks for having me, Lenny. I'm really excited to talk about this because onboarding is one of those things that everyone knows is important, but very few teams actually invest in properly.

Lenny: Let's start with the basics. Why is onboarding so important?

Lauryn Isford: So the data is really clear on this. If you look at the best SaaS companies, the ones that have really strong retention, they almost always have invested deeply in onboarding. The reason is simple: the first experience a user has with your product determines whether they'll ever come back. We found at Airtable that users who completed our onboarding flow were three times more likely to still be active 30 days later compared to those who skipped it.

Lenny: Three times! That's a huge difference. What does a good onboarding flow look like?

Lauryn Isford: There are a few key principles. First, you need to get users to their "aha moment" as quickly as possible. At Notion, we found that the aha moment was when a user created their first page and shared it with a teammate. Everything in the onboarding was designed to get them there. Second, you need to reduce friction relentlessly. Every extra step you add to onboarding reduces completion rates. We measured this at Airtable — each additional step in our onboarding wizard reduced completion by about 20 percent. Third, personalization matters. Different users come to your product for different reasons. At Airtable, a project manager has very different needs from a marketer. We built branching onboarding paths based on the user's role and primary use case.

Lenny: Can you walk us through how you actually measure onboarding effectiveness?

Lauryn Isford: Absolutely. The most important metric is what I call "activation rate" — the percentage of new signups who reach your defined activation milestone within a given time window. At Airtable, our activation milestone was "created a base with at least 3 records and invited one collaborator within 7 days." We tracked this religiously and ran experiments every sprint to improve it. We also looked at time-to-activation, which is how long it takes users to reach that milestone. Shorter is almost always better. We went from a median of 4 days to under 2 days by simplifying our template selection flow.

Lenny: What are the biggest mistakes teams make with onboarding?

Lauryn Isford: The number one mistake is treating onboarding as a one-time project rather than an ongoing program. The best teams I've seen have a dedicated onboarding squad that runs experiments continuously. At Notion, the onboarding team ran about 15 experiments per quarter. Another common mistake is trying to show users everything your product can do. Users don't need a product tour of every feature. They need to accomplish one meaningful thing. Finally, many teams don't instrument their onboarding properly. You need to track every step, every drop-off, every click. Without that data, you're flying blind.

Lenny: What about B2B products where onboarding might involve a whole team?

Lauryn Isford: Great question. For B2B products, you need to think about onboarding at two levels: individual user onboarding and team onboarding. The individual needs to understand how to use the product. But the team needs to understand how the product fits into their workflow. At Airtable, we found that the single biggest predictor of team retention was whether the team had a "champion" — someone who was deeply invested in making Airtable work for the team. So part of our onboarding strategy was identifying and nurturing those champions early. We'd give them extra resources, personal outreach, and advanced training.

Lenny: This is incredible. What frameworks do you recommend for thinking about onboarding?

Lauryn Isford: I like to think about onboarding in three phases. Phase one is the "Setup" phase — getting the user's account configured, importing data, connecting integrations. Phase two is the "Aha" phase — getting them to experience the core value of your product. Phase three is the "Habit" phase — helping them build a regular pattern of usage. Most teams over-invest in phase one and under-invest in phases two and three. The setup phase should be as minimal as possible. The aha phase is where you should spend most of your engineering effort. And the habit phase is where things like email sequences, in-app prompts, and usage reports become really valuable. One more thing: always have a "skip" option. Some users know exactly what they want to do and don't need hand-holding. Let them get straight to the product.
