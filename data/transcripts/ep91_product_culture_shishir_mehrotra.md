Lenny: Today's guest is Shishir Mehrotra, the CEO and co-founder of Coda, and previously VP of Product at YouTube. We're going to talk about what it takes to build a world-class product culture. Shishir, welcome.

Shishir Mehrotra: Thanks, Lenny. This is a topic I've been thinking about for over 15 years now, across YouTube, Coda, and the many companies I've advised.

Lenny: What does "product culture" even mean?

Shishir Mehrotra: Product culture is the set of shared beliefs, practices, and rituals that determine how a company builds products. It's the operating system of your product team. At YouTube, our product culture was very data-driven. Every decision had to be backed by metrics. At Coda, our culture is more conviction-driven — we make bold bets based on our vision of how work should change. Neither approach is inherently better. What matters is that the culture is intentional and consistent. The worst product cultures are the ones that are accidental — where different teams operate with different assumptions and there's no shared understanding of how decisions get made.

Lenny: How do you actually build a strong product culture?

Shishir Mehrotra: I think about it in terms of three pillars. First, rituals — the recurring meetings, reviews, and processes that shape how work gets done. At Coda, our most important ritual is the weekly "Dory" — a document where anyone in the company can submit questions, and the leadership team answers them publicly. This creates transparency and alignment. Second, artifacts — the documents, templates, and frameworks that encode your product thinking. At Coda, every product decision starts with what we call a "One Pager" — a structured document that forces clear thinking about the problem, the proposed solution, the metrics for success, and the risks. Third, heroes — the stories you tell about great product decisions in your company's history. Every company has moments where someone made a brilliant product call. Celebrating those stories teaches everyone what "good" looks like.

Lenny: Can you talk about the concept of "rituals" more?

Shishir Mehrotra: Rituals are incredibly powerful because they create predictability and rhythm. Here are some rituals I've found essential. First, a weekly product review where the leadership team looks at the most important metrics and discusses what's changing. Second, a monthly roadmap review where PMs present their plans and get cross-functional feedback. Third, quarterly planning where the team steps back and aligns on the big bets for the next quarter. Fourth, regular "customer immersion" sessions where the entire product team watches customer calls or reads customer feedback together. The key is consistency. A ritual only works if it happens every time, on schedule, with a clear format. If your product review gets canceled half the time, it's not a ritual — it's an aspiration.

Lenny: What role does leadership play in product culture?

Shishir Mehrotra: The CEO sets the product culture, whether they intend to or not. If the CEO constantly overrides product decisions based on gut feeling, you'll get a culture where PMs learn to wait for top-down direction instead of making their own calls. If the CEO asks thoughtful questions and empowers PMs to make decisions, you'll get a culture of ownership and initiative. At YouTube, Susan Wojcicki was incredible at this. She would challenge our thinking but ultimately trust the PM to make the call. At Coda, I try to model the behavior I want to see. I write my own one-pagers, I participate in design reviews, I use our own product for my daily work. People watch what leaders do, not what they say. The most important thing a leader can do is demonstrate the product culture they want to create.

Lenny: What about hiring for product culture?

Shishir Mehrotra: Hiring is where culture gets built or destroyed. I look for three things when hiring PMs. First, intellectual curiosity — do they ask great questions? Do they naturally want to understand why things work the way they do? Second, builder instinct — do they love making things? The best PMs I've hired were people who built side projects, wrote newsletters, or created tools for their communities. Third, communication clarity — can they take a complex idea and explain it simply? This is the single most important PM skill, and it's shockingly rare. One technique I use in interviews is what I call the "explain this" test. I ask candidates to explain a product they admire as if they were talking to someone who's never used it. The best candidates can do this in under two minutes with remarkable clarity.

Lenny: How do you maintain product culture as you scale?

Shishir Mehrotra: This is the hardest part. Culture naturally dilutes as you grow. There are a few things I've found helpful. First, codify your principles. Write them down. At Coda, we have a document called "How We Build Products" that every new PM reads on their first day. Second, create mentorship structures. Every new PM should be paired with an experienced PM who embodies the culture. Third, be willing to make hard calls. If someone is technically excellent but working against the product culture, that's a problem you need to address. Culture isn't just what you celebrate — it's also what you're willing to tolerate. Fourth, invest in onboarding. The first 30 days of a PM's tenure shape their understanding of the culture more than anything else. Make those days count.
