Lenny: I'm excited to welcome Shreyas Doshi, who was a PM leader at Stripe, Twitter, Google, and Yahoo. We're going to talk about the art and science of prioritization, which I think is one of the most underrated PM skills. Shreyas, welcome.

Shreyas Doshi: Lenny, thanks for having me. Prioritization is something I think about almost obsessively because I've seen so many teams — including my own — get it wrong in ways that cost them months or even years of progress.

Lenny: Let's dive right in. What does good prioritization look like?

Shreyas Doshi: Good prioritization starts with clarity about what game you're playing. There are fundamentally different prioritization strategies depending on your company's stage. At an early-stage startup, prioritization is about finding product-market fit. You should be doing the minimum number of things that let you learn whether your core hypothesis is correct. At a growth-stage company, prioritization is about scaling what works. And at a mature company, prioritization is about optimizing and defending your position. The mistake most PMs make is applying a mature-company prioritization framework to an early-stage startup. Using RICE scoring to decide what to build when you have 100 users is almost always wrong.

Lenny: I love that. Can you talk about your LNO framework?

Shreyas Doshi: Sure. LNO stands for Leverage, Neutral, and Overhead. Every task a PM does falls into one of these categories. Leverage tasks are the ones that produce disproportionate results. Writing a great strategy document that aligns the entire team for a quarter — that's leverage work. Neutral tasks are things that need to get done but produce proportional results. Filing a bug ticket is neutral work. Overhead tasks are things that don't directly produce results but are necessary for the organization to function. Attending status meetings is overhead. The key insight is this: most PMs spend way too much time on neutral and overhead work, and not nearly enough on leverage work. I recommend PMs audit their calendar and task list weekly and ask: what percentage of my time last week was spent on leverage work? If it's below 30 percent, something needs to change.

Lenny: How should an early-stage PM approach prioritization specifically?

Shreyas Doshi: For early-stage PMs, I have three principles. First, ruthlessly limit work in progress. You should never have more than two or three major initiatives at once. If you're working on five things, you're not working on anything effectively. Second, prioritize learning over output. The goal at the early stage isn't to ship features — it's to learn what works. Sometimes the highest-priority thing is running an experiment that might tell you NOT to build something. That's incredibly valuable. Third, use what I call "conviction-based prioritization." Not everything can be validated with data at the early stage. Sometimes you need to make a bet based on your conviction about where the market is heading. The best early-stage PMs I've worked with have strong intuition AND the ability to validate that intuition quickly.

Lenny: What about stakeholder management in prioritization?

Shreyas Doshi: This is where the political dimension of PM work comes in. One of the hardest things about prioritization is saying no to powerful stakeholders. I've found that the most effective approach is what I call "transparent prioritization." Make your prioritization framework visible to everyone. Show people exactly how you're making decisions and what the trade-offs are. When a VP asks you to prioritize their pet project, you can show them the framework and say, "Here's where it ranks relative to everything else. If you want us to do this, what should we drop?" This changes the conversation from "Why won't you build my thing?" to "Given our constraints, what's the best use of our limited engineering resources?" It also helps to share your roadmap widely and regularly. When people understand the full picture of what the team is working on and why, they're much more likely to accept that their request can't be accommodated right now.

Lenny: Any other frameworks you'd recommend?

Shreyas Doshi: I'm a big fan of the "one-way door vs. two-way door" concept that Amazon popularized. One-way door decisions are irreversible or very costly to reverse — like choosing your core technology platform or your pricing model. These deserve deep analysis and broad input. Two-way door decisions are easily reversible — like the color of a button or the exact wording of a notification. These should be made quickly with minimal process. The problem is that most organizations treat every decision like a one-way door, which creates unnecessary bureaucracy and slows everything down. As a PM, one of the highest-leverage things you can do is correctly classify decisions and push your team to move fast on two-way doors. Another framework I love is "opportunity cost thinking." Every time you decide to build something, you're also deciding NOT to build everything else you could have built with those resources. The question isn't just "Is this a good idea?" It's "Is this the BEST use of our limited resources right now?" That subtle shift in framing changes everything.
